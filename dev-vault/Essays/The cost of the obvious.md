# The Cost of the Obvious

Much of the tension in making things comes from deciding what deserves to be obvious. Every screen, every paragraph, every product has room for only a few things that a person sees without trying. Everything else has to wait its turn.

It's tempting to make everything obvious. But when everything shouts, nothing is heard. You end up with a page like a thumbtack board, covered edge to edge, where the one note that matters is pinned somewhere under the rest.

So the real work is ranking. Not by importance in the abstract, but by how often someone needs a thing and how much it costs them to look for it. The common path should be effortless. The rare path should be possible. And the path almost nobody takes can live behind a door, as long as the door has a sign.

This isn't the same as prioritizing a backlog. A backlog tells you what to build next. Ranking tells you where things go once they exist. A feature can be important and still belong in the back, because the people who need it will go looking for it anyway.

Writing works the same way. The first sentence of a paragraph is the obvious part. It carries the claim. The sentences after it are easy to reach if the reader wants them, and the footnotes are possible for the few who care. When I bury the claim in the middle, I am asking the reader to do my sorting for me.

I think the hardest part is that ranking means saying no to good things. A clever aside, a second example, a sentence I like the sound of. They are not wrong. They are just not obvious-worthy, and leaving them in costs the reader a little attention each time.

The fix is rarely to delete. Usually it is to move. Put the claim up front. Push the aside down. Give the rare thing a quiet place to live. The piece gets shorter to read without getting shorter to write.
